How to set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC in Microsoft 365

By Avinash Sahu · 13 November 2026 · 8 min read

If your email lands in spam, or someone sends mail that pretends to be your domain, the cause is usually missing email authentication. Three DNS-based checks fix most of it: SPF, DKIM and DMARC. This guide is for a Microsoft 365 tenant with its own domain. You need admin access to Microsoft 365 and to wherever your domain's DNS is hosted (often GoDaddy, Cloudflare or your registrar).

What each one does

CheckIn one sentence
SPFLists which servers are allowed to send mail for your domain.
DKIMAdds a digital signature so receivers can verify the message really came from your domain and was not changed.
DMARCTells receivers what to do when SPF and DKIM fail, and sends you reports about it.

Before you start

  1. Confirm your domain is verified in the Microsoft 365 admin center (Settings, then Domains).
  2. Write down every service that sends email as your domain: newsletter tool, CRM, invoicing, website forms, printers.
  3. Have the login for your DNS host ready.

Step 1: SPF

Add one TXT record on the root of your domain (host @):

v=spf1 include:spf.protection.outlook.com -all

If other services also send as your domain, add their include: entries before -all. You can only have one SPF record per domain. Two records break SPF, so edit the existing one instead of adding a second.

Step 2: DKIM

  1. Open the Microsoft Defender portal (security.microsoft.com), then Email & collaboration, Policies & rules, Threat policies, Email authentication settings, and choose the DKIM tab.
  2. Select your domain. Microsoft shows two CNAME records: selector1._domainkey and selector2._domainkey.
  3. Add both as CNAME records at your DNS host. Many hosts add your domain automatically, so in the Host field you usually enter only selector1._domainkey, not the full name.
  4. Wait for DNS to update. This can take from a few minutes to several hours.
  5. Go back to the DKIM tab and switch on Sign messages for this domain with DKIM signatures.
If you see "CNAME record does not exist": DNS has not updated yet, or the Host field has your domain twice (for example selector1._domainkey.yourdomain.com.yourdomain.com). Fix the host, wait, and try again.

You can also do it with PowerShell in Exchange Online:

Connect-ExchangeOnline
Get-DkimSigningConfig -Identity yourdomain.com | Format-List Selector1CNAME,Selector2CNAME
Set-DkimSigningConfig -Identity yourdomain.com -Enabled $true

Step 3: DMARC

Add a TXT record with the host _dmarc:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@yourdomain.com

Start with p=none. It only watches and reports, so no legitimate mail is blocked. Read the reports for two to four weeks (they are XML files, and several services offer free report readers). Fix any legitimate sender that fails. Then move to p=quarantine, and later to p=reject once everything passes.

Step 4: Test it

Send an email from your domain to a Gmail address. Open the message, choose Show original, and look for SPF: PASS, DKIM: PASS and DMARC: PASS.

Common mistakes

  • Two SPF records on the same domain.
  • Forgetting a third-party sender (newsletter tool, CRM) and then moving DMARC to reject, which blocks your own mail.
  • Turning on DKIM before the CNAME records resolve.
  • Typing the full domain into a Host field that already adds it.
  • Jumping straight to p=reject without watching reports first.
  • Using a rua address that does not exist, so you never see the reports.

From my own work

In a Microsoft 365 tenant I took over, DKIM was not set up and DMARC was monitor-only. I turned on DKIM first, left DMARC at monitor-only to watch the reports, and planned the move to stricter policies after that. Going in this order keeps real mail flowing while you tighten things.
